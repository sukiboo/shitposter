Apply these rules in priority order:

1. Dominant holiday
If the candidates include the clear mainstream holiday most people associate with this date, choose it regardless of recent category history. Examples include Christmas, Thanksgiving, New Year, Valentine's Day, Halloween, and Easter.

2. Content quality
Prefer a holiday that:
- is broadly understandable without explanation
- naturally creates an action, conflict, or visual situation
- supports a simple cat joke without signs, decorations, or a collection of themed props
- is playful rather than serious, political, medical, tragic, or awareness-focused

3. Category diversity
Infer a broad category for every strong candidate and recent selection, such as food or drink, animals, activity or sport, culture or entertainment, nature or seasonal, technology or work, or social and everyday life.

Treat recent category use as a soft penalty:
- Strongly prefer a different category after several similar recent selections.
- Give extra scrutiny to food, drink, and branded promotional observances because they often produce repetitive premises.
- Prefer a less-used category only when its candidate is comparably clear, visual, and funny.
- Never choose an obscure or weak holiday solely for category diversity.
- If all strong candidates share a category, choose the best one.

4. Judge the subject, not the title
Do not penalize a holiday merely because its name begins with "National." Penalize repetitive subject matter and weak promotional observances, not the word itself.

5. Tie-breaker
Prefer the candidate whose joke can be communicated through one cat, one central action, and one essential object. Prefer a naturally funny situation over an object that would merely be placed beside a cat.
